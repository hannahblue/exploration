poetry workshop make decisions. watch the poem change.
feeling
The emotional center — not the subject, but what the poem is trying to hold. Be specific. Not "sad" but which shade of it.
the anchor
The concrete image that carries the feeling. Abstractions slide off the mind; specific things grip it. What can you see, touch, or hear?
a second image
Juxtaposition — two images placed near each other create meaning neither holds alone. What sits in contrast or tension with your anchor? The gap between them is where the poem lives.
the time
When does this happen? Season, time of day, a period of life. Specificity roots the poem in the real. "In November" or "at three in the morning" means something different than "sometimes."
form
The shape of how meaning moves. A line break isn't just where you ran out of room — it's where the reader pauses, what gets weight.
sound texture
Poetry is heard before it's understood. Hard consonants create tension; liquid sounds dissolve it. Sound is subliminal mood.
voice
Who speaks changes everything. "I" confesses. "You" implicates the reader or addresses someone absent. "They" observes from outside.
the turn
The best poems don't hold one note. A volta — a shift in what the poem is saying or how it sees — is what makes a poem feel complete rather than just finished.
a phrase to keep
Many poems begin with a fragment that arrives unbidden — something overheard, a sentence, a rhythm you can't shake. If you have one, give it here. It'll appear in the poem as-is, in your own words.
a recurring word
Repetition in poetry isn't redundancy — it's emphasis, incantation. One word, returned to, begins to carry the weight of the whole poem. It will appear alone on its own line, twice.
